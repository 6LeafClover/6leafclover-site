# The Contradiction

Brindley Software Group ran an annual partner satisfaction survey, and co-selling with the vendor's own sales team consistently ranked as a top-three priority among respondents. And yet, when we looked at actual deal data, co-sell involvement on partner-sourced opportunities was under 8%. The stated intention and the observed behaviour didn't match.

# Understanding Why

Rather than assuming partners were being polite in the survey, we ran structured conversations with a cross-section of the partner base to understand the disconnect. Three things came up repeatedly:

- Partners genuinely didn't know how to formally bring the vendor into a deal — there was no defined process, just an informal "ask your account manager"
- Nobody had ever clarified who did what once a deal became joint, so partners feared duplicated effort or, worse, conflicting messages to the customer
- Underneath both of these was a quieter fear: that looping in the vendor meant risking the relationship with "their" customer

# What We Built

We treated this as a design problem, not a motivation problem:

- A simple co-sell registration process, taking under two minutes, triggering a defined internal response within 48 hours
- A written playbook setting out exactly who leads which part of a joint deal — commercial ownership, technical demonstration, contracting — removing the ambiguity partners had been navigating informally
- An explicit, written commitment that the partner retained the primary commercial relationship with the customer throughout, addressing the underlying fear directly rather than hoping it would go away

# The Result

Co-sell registrations rose roughly fourfold within six months. Partner satisfaction scores on the co-selling question rose too — not because sentiment had changed, but because the process finally matched the sentiment that had been there all along.

# The Lesson

When partners consistently express interest in something and consistently don't act on it, the gap is rarely a lack of desire. It's usually friction, ambiguity, or an unaddressed fear sitting quietly underneath the process. Fix the mechanism, and the stated priority and the real behaviour tend to come back into line.
