## The setup

Corvine Systems (one of our customers, the names are changed) ran a partner compensation model built almost entirely around new logo registration. Register a deal, close it, take the margin. Simple, and easy to administer.

The trouble was what it produced. Partners had no financial reason to care what happened after the sale. Adoption of the product was patchy. Renewals were being lost quietly, month after month, with nobody accountable for catching them.

## The diagnosis

This wasn't a partner engagement problem, and it wasn't a product problem. It was an incentive design problem. Partners were behaving exactly as the compensation model told them to. Asking them to "care more" about renewals, without paying them for it, was never going to work.

## The rebuild

We redesigned the model around the full lifecycle of a customer relationship, not just its opening moment:

- **Registration** — rewarded, but at a reduced weighting
- **Successful onboarding and adoption** — a new, meaningful component
- **Renewal** — directly tied to partner payout
- **Expansion revenue** — the highest-weighted category of all

This wasn't about spending more on incentives. It was about redirecting the same budget toward the outcomes that actually mattered to the business.

## What changed

Within a year, partner-influenced renewal rates rose by 18 percentage points. Partners began proactively checking in with customers ahead of renewal dates — something the old model gave them no reason to do. Several partners started building expansion-focused sales motions of their own, because for the first time, it paid to.

## The lesson

Compensation is not a thank-you note. It's an instruction, and partners are very good at following instructions precisely. If a partner programme is producing behaviour leadership doesn't want, the model is usually working exactly as designed — just designed for the wrong outcome.
