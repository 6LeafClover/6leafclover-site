# What Happened

Calderwood Mutual built a fast, well-engineered automated underwriting workflow. Technically, it performed exactly as intended. Three weeks before the planned go-live, the compliance function reviewed it for the first time and identified a significant gap: the audit trail the system generated didn't meet the regulatory record-keeping standard required for underwriting decisions.

Fixing it retroactively meant reworking core parts of the data logging architecture, and the go-live slipped by several weeks — entirely avoidable, had the requirement been known at the design stage rather than discovered at the review stage.

# Why It Happened

This wasn't a case of the compliance team being slow or unhelpful. It was a case of compliance being invited into the process too late to influence it, at a point where their feedback could only ever mean rework rather than design guidance. The programme had been built around technical and operational stakeholders, with compliance treated as a final sign-off rather than a contributor.

# What Changed For The Next Phase

- **A standing seat for compliance** in the weekly programme governance meetings, from the very start of the next automation workstream — not invited occasionally, but present by default
- **Audit trail and record-keeping requirements written as functional specifications**, alongside the operational requirements, before any development began
- **A compliance sign-off built into the design phase**, not just the pre-launch phase, so gaps could be caught and addressed while they were still cheap to fix

# The Result

The next automated workflow launched on its original schedule and passed its compliance review in a single pass, with no rework required. The approach became the standard operating model for every subsequent automation project at Calderwood.

# The Lesson

In a regulated environment, compliance requirements aren't a constraint to be checked at the end. They're a design input, exactly like performance or usability, and belong in the room from the first workshop. Brought in early, they barely slow a programme down. Brought in late, they can stop it at the finish line.
