# The Situation

Saint in the City Automation, a mid-market technology company, had just completed the acquisition of a smaller competitor, Instep Software. Both businesses had partner programmes of their own — different tiers, different margins, different rules of engagement. Neither had been built with the other in mind.

Within weeks, the practical problems surfaced. Partners from the acquired business didn't know whether their existing deals would still be honoured under the old terms. Some partners overlapped with Weybridge's existing channel in the same territory, raising immediate questions about who now owned which accounts. A handful of the acquired company's stronger partners, uncertain and unsettled, began quietly exploring relationships with competitors instead.

# What We Did

We treated partner integration as its own workstream, run in parallel with the wider commercial integration, rather than as an afterthought to be sorted out once the bigger systems questions were settled.

**Weeks 1–2: Mapping.** A full audit of both partner estates — revenue, active deals, territory, contractual terms — to understand exactly where overlap and conflict existed before making any decisions.

**Weeks 3–6: Protection.** Active deals in flight were explicitly protected under their original terms, and every affected partner received direct, specific communication about what this meant for them, rather than a generic "welcome to the family" announcement.

**Weeks 7–10: Harmonisation.** Commercial terms were brought into line, with a clear, published transition timeline so no partner was surprised by a change.

**Weeks 11–13: Unification.** Only once trust had been re-established did we merge the two programmes into a single structure.

# The Result

Partner attrition in the acquired business's channel came in under 5%, against an internal planning assumption closer to 20%. Several of the partners who had been quietly exploring alternatives stayed, specifically citing the clarity and speed of communication as the reason.

# The Lesson

In an acquisition, partners are watching for signals long before anyone officially tells them anything. Sequencing the integration — protect, then harmonise, then unify — and communicating honestly at every stage costs relatively little. Losing partners because nobody got round to telling them what was happening costs a great deal more.
